# Employee Politics Field Guide

This guide covers everyday workplace influence, defensive tactics and organizational power. It is written to help an employee or manager become more effective and less vulnerable—not to teach deception, sabotage or coercion.

## 1. The everyday power map

For a decision, identify the people who control:

- **Formal authority:** approval, hiring, evaluation, budget, policy or sign-off.
- **Access:** who gets a meeting, customer conversation, agenda slot or executive briefing.
- **Information:** who knows the actual timeline, history, customer risk, financial constraint or reason for a change.
- **Expertise:** who can validate or invalidate the proposal.
- **Implementation:** who must do the work after the decision.
- **Legitimacy:** who is trusted by the people affected.
- **Veto or friction:** who can delay or introduce a legitimate compliance, capacity or safety constraint.

Do not assign a “political score” to a person based on gossip. Map the decision and each role’s observable influence.

## 2. Twenty-six recurring patterns and responses

### 1. Credit stealing
**Signals:** Shared work is repeatedly described as a single person’s deliverable; authorship is omitted from decks or review notes.  
**Response:** Make contributions visible before the executive meeting; use shared authorship and evidence; correct inaccuracies neutrally and privately first when safe.

### 2. Blame transfer
**Signals:** A failed outcome is explained without the preceding decision, dependency or scope change.  
**Response:** Reconstruct a factual timeline and separate decision, execution, dependency and external cause. Own your part without accepting false ownership of another decision.

### 3. Gatekeeping
**Signals:** One person is the only route to documents, customers, meetings or approvals.  
**Response:** Ask for the process and access criteria; introduce a shared source of truth; document the dependency and operational risk.

### 4. Shadow decision-maker
**Signals:** Formal leader waits for an informal figure’s approval.  
**Response:** Learn the actual governance without undermining it. Ask whose approval is required and whether delegated authority can be made explicit.

### 5. Silent veto
**Signals:** Repeated delay without a direct rejection.  
**Response:** Ask what missing condition blocks the decision, who can resolve it, and what date is realistic. Give a “yes if / no because” path.

### 6. Triangulation
**Signals:** Two colleagues receive inconsistent claims about what a third person said.  
**Response:** Verify with the source in a neutral shared channel. Do not repeat allegations as facts or recruit a faction.

### 7. Favouritism
**Signals:** Similar requests receive different treatment without an explained criterion.  
**Response:** Compare like-for-like cases; ask about the governing standard; raise process consistency rather than attacking someone’s relationship.

### 8. Promotion opacity
**Signals:** Success criteria are vague, shifting or not communicated until after evaluation.  
**Response:** Request written competencies and examples; create an evidence portfolio; ask for gaps and the review date.

### 9. Performance-review ambush
**Signals:** New criticisms appear only during appraisal or PIP.  
**Response:** Ask for dated examples, measurable expectations, support and a fair checkpoint. Keep factual notes and responses.

### 10. Exclusion from meetings
**Signals:** Decisions affecting your deliverable are made without the people responsible for execution.  
**Response:** Request inclusion based on a specific dependency or send a concise pre-read and questions. If exclusion continues, escalate the decision risk—not the insult.

### 11. Overloading / impossible deadlines
**Signals:** Work is assigned without resource trade-offs, then delay is attributed to the employee.  
**Response:** Present capacity and options: what can be delivered by when, what must be deprioritized, and which risk the decision owner accepts.

### 12. Vague “urgent” requests
**Signals:** Constant urgency bypasses review or safety.  
**Response:** Ask for the deadline driver and minimum acceptable review. A legitimate emergency may change process sequencing but does not justify falsification or illegal conduct.

### 13. Verbal-only commitment
**Signals:** Material changes are discussed but later denied.  
**Response:** Send a succinct neutral recap with decision, owner, date and next action. For sensitive matters, use authorized records and approved channels.

### 14. Boss shopping
**Signals:** The same issue is taken to multiple senior leaders until someone gives a favorable answer.  
**Response:** Clarify the designated decision owner and escalation route; make previous decisions visible; avoid using access to create conflicting approvals.

### 15. Factional alignment
**Signals:** Colleagues demand loyalty to a person rather than an objective.  
**Response:** Stay courteous, deliver on shared work, avoid gossip, and state that you will align with the agreed business decision.

### 16. Meeting theatre
**Signals:** Public discussion is symbolic because the actual consultation occurred earlier.  
**Response:** Learn the legitimate pre-alignment process; send evidence early; ask what decision remains open. Do not manufacture consensus or misstate who agreed.

### 17. Moving goalposts
**Signals:** Success criteria shift after delivery.  
**Response:** Version the scope, criteria and assumptions. Ask who authorized the change and what should now move in date, budget or acceptance.

### 18. Information hoarding
**Signals:** Relevant facts are withheld to preserve indispensability.  
**Response:** Make dependencies explicit, create shared documentation and reduce single-person risk. Do not access data without authorization.

### 19. False consensus
**Signals:** A leader says “everyone agrees” but dissenting views are not represented.  
**Response:** Ask which functions were consulted, what unresolved risks remain, and whether dissent can be recorded without retaliation.

### 20. Retaliation risk
**Signals:** Negative treatment follows a complaint, safety report, lawful leave, protected disclosure or refusal of improper instruction.  
**Response:** Preserve authorized contemporaneous evidence, consult policy and qualified independent advice, and use a protected channel. Do not treat retaliation as a normal negotiation problem.

### 21. Rumour / reputational attack
**Signals:** Claims spread without source, date or evidence.  
**Response:** Do not amplify. Correct concrete falsehoods through proportionate channels, retain relevant evidence, and seek legal/HR advice for serious defamation or harassment.

### 22. Micromanagement
**Signals:** Approval is demanded for low-risk actions while accountability remains with the employee.  
**Response:** Propose thresholds, status cadence, risk-based approvals and decision logs. Avoid framing all oversight as personal distrust.

### 23. Personal favour exchange
**Signals:** Access, promotion or a business benefit appears linked to a private favour.  
**Response:** Set a clear boundary; do not trade confidential information or misuse authority. For bribery, harassment or conflicts, use the relevant compliance route.

### 24. Owner or promoter override
**Signals:** An owner changes a manager’s decision without a documented rationale.  
**Response:** Confirm the new business decision and its consequences; request clarity on delegation for future matters; do not pretend the earlier commitment still applies.

### 25. Exit pressure / forced resignation
**Signals:** Employee is pressured to resign without clear reasons or fair process.  
**Response:** Do not sign under pressure without understanding terms. Ask for written proposals, preserve lawful records, review contract and local law with qualified advice, and use appropriate grievance mechanisms.

### 26. Contractor / permanent employee divide
**Signals:** Workers doing similar tasks have different pay, benefits, security or grievance access due to employment category.  
**Response:** Distinguish the formal contract and real working conditions; use a qualified HR/labour-law assessment; avoid unsupported claims of legal violation; ensure the worker voice is heard.

## 3. Scripts that preserve dignity and create a record

### Clarifying a priority conflict
“Both requests are important. With current capacity, I can complete A by Friday or B by Friday, but not both to the required standard. Which one should take priority, and what should move?”

### Correcting an omitted contribution
“Thanks for sharing the update. For accuracy, I led the analysis and drafted the recommendation, with data support from X and review from Y. The analysis changed the projected cost from A to B. I’ll add the contribution summary to the next status note.”

### Asking for promotion criteria
“I would like to understand the criteria for the next level so I can plan against them. Could we agree on the required outcomes, evidence of readiness, the remaining gaps and a review date?”

### Confirming a verbal decision
“To make sure I implement this correctly, my notes are: decision A; owner B; timeline C; risk/trade-off D. Please let me know if I have misunderstood any part.”

### Responding to an alleged instruction from a third party
“I’m happy to help. Since this changes the agreed priority, can we confirm with the decision owner which commitment should move?”

### Challenging a questionable shortcut
“I understand the timing pressure. I can’t approve or record it in a way that bypasses the required control. A compliant alternative is X; it would take Y and preserve the audit trail.”

### Asking a silent blocker for the condition to proceed
“What is the one condition or piece of evidence that is preventing a decision? If we can address it by [date], would you be comfortable deciding then?”

### Responding to a factional invitation
“I value working with everyone involved. I’m going to focus on the agreed outcome and make sure the decision, risks and responsibilities are clear.”

## 4. Personal political-resilience system

Maintain four small artifacts, using only information you are authorized to store:

1. **Outcome log:** major work, contribution, measurable result and collaborators.
2. **Decision log:** decision, owner, date, rationale, assumptions, next step and unresolved risk.
3. **Stakeholder map:** role, legitimate interest, work dependency, engagement preference and confidence in each inference.
4. **Risk/learning log:** recurring process gaps, what you tried, outcome, and what to test next.

Avoid collecting personal secrets, private information, unverified gossip or unauthorized copies of confidential data. A useful record documents work and decisions; it is not an intelligence file on colleagues.

## 5. When not to “play politics”

Do not solve serious fraud, threats, assault, sexual harassment, safety risk, discrimination, retaliation, bribery or unlawful instructions through informal influence alone. Use the appropriate investigation, emergency, HR, union, regulator, legal or protected-reporting route. Do not use deception, unauthorized surveillance, secret dossiers, blackmail, threats, public shaming, impersonation, planted rumours, or sabotage.
