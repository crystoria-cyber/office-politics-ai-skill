# CPS Research Casebook: Corporate Power, Workplace Politics & Indian Business

**Version:** 2.0 | **Research cut-off:** 27 October 2026  
**Purpose:** A source-linked casebook for an AI assistant learning to analyze real-world corporate power, owner-led businesses, boardroom conflict, employee politics, labour relations, governance failures, whistleblowing and organizational change.

## How to read this casebook

This is a learning resource, not a verdict on every person or company mentioned. Each case separates (1) verifiable public events, (2) claims made by a party or regulator, and (3) analytical lessons. An allegation is not a finding; an investigation is not a conviction; a dispute being settled does not establish that either side was right. Some case materials are academic teaching cases that use an anonymized or composite company; those are clearly marked and are not represented as public-company scandals.

The casebook does **not** assume all Indian companies, family firms, or owner-led SMEs behave alike. “Lala company” is colloquial shorthand in this pack for a business where an owner/promoter or family circle has unusually centralized control and informal rules may be more influential than the organization chart. It is not a formal company category and should not be used as a stereotype or as proof that a firm is unethical.

## A. Boardroom, promoter, founder and family power

### Case 1 — Tata Sons / Cyrus Mistry: owner influence, professional CEO authority and board process (2016–2021)

**Public record.** Cyrus Mistry was removed as Tata Sons chair in October 2016. The dispute moved through the National Company Law Tribunal and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal. In March 2021, the Supreme Court allowed the Tata companies’ appeals and set aside the NCLAT order that had restored Mistry. The judgment is the primary legal source; news chronologies are useful for orientation only.

**Politics to study.** Formal board authority, shareholder rights, the difference between operating leadership and ownership influence, competing narratives about strategy and governance, and how litigation changes bargaining power. A public dispute does not by itself establish that all claims by one side were true.

**Skill lessons.** Build separate maps for (a) formal decision rights, (b) economic control, (c) access to trusted owners, (d) reputation with stakeholders, and (e) legal options. In a leadership dispute, the board’s process, the company’s articles, shareholder agreements and contemporaneous records matter more than a simplified “who is powerful” story. Avoid treating informal influence as inherently improper; test it against disclosed governance rules and the evidence.

**Sources:** Supreme Court judgment hosted by Indian Kanoon: https://indiankanoon.org/doc/5416696/ ; chronology: https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/india/chronology-of-events-in-tata-mistry-case/articleshow/81702963.cms

### Case 2 — Infosys founders versus CEO Vishal Sikka: the founder shadow (2017)

**Public record.** Infosys’ founders, led publicly by N. R. Narayana Murthy, questioned decisions about executive pay, severance and governance. The company and CEO disputed aspects of the criticism. Vishal Sikka resigned in August 2017 after a prolonged public disagreement, citing recurring disruption; company leadership attributed the pressure to the public attacks by the founder. Reuters reporting documented both the dispute and the immediate leadership transition.

**Politics to study.** A founder can retain informal legitimacy and influence after operational authority is delegated. A professionally appointed CEO may have formal authority but still need a stable mandate from the board and controlling stakeholders. Public messaging can become a proxy contest over who defines the company’s values.

**Skill lessons.** Before taking a senior role, clarify the mandate, board alignment, non-negotiable decision rights, escalation route and founder/owner involvement. Where two power centres disagree, seek a documented decision process rather than trying to win through repeated media or corridor narratives. In employee cases, do not assume the loudest public narrative is a complete account.

**Source:** Reuters reporting syndicated by Business Standard: https://www.business-standard.com/article/reuters/infosys-ceo-vishal-sikka-resigns-co-names-interim-chief-executive-117081800294_1.html

### Case 3 — Infosys anonymous whistleblower complaints: claim, investigation and evidence (2019–2020)

**Public record.** Anonymous complaints alleged improper practices connected with large deals and approvals by senior executives. Infosys said the audit committee had referred the matter for independent investigation. In its subsequent public disclosure, the company stated that the investigators found no evidence supporting the new allegations and described evidence it said rebutted them. That is the publicly reported outcome from the company’s investigation disclosure; it should not be confused with a court adjudication or with proof that every aspect of corporate governance was beyond question.

**Politics to study.** Anonymous complaints can serve a legitimate accountability purpose, but can also be incomplete or mistaken. Seniority, incentives, internal review gates, investor confidence, leaks and reputational risk interact. The response to an allegation is itself a governance test.

**Skill lessons.** Preserve original evidence, distinguish first-hand knowledge from inference, avoid editing records, use the official vigil/whistleblowing channel, protect confidentiality, prohibit retaliation, and allow a genuinely independent review. Avoid treating a complaint as established fact or attacking the complainant as disloyal. A manager should not investigate their own alleged misconduct.

**Sources:** Infosys investigation disclosure: https://www.infosys.com/newsroom/press-releases/documents/audit-committee-finds-no-evidence-financial-impropriety.pdf ; Indian Express timeline and initial response: https://indianexpress.com/article/business/companies/infosys-places-whistleblower-complaint-before-audit-panel-6081197/

### Case 4 — Godrej family settlement and division of business groups (2024)

**Public record.** A family settlement was publicly disclosed to stock exchanges in April 2024. The arrangement separated the Godrej businesses into two independently operated groups while both retained the Godrej brand, subject to the terms announced publicly. This is an example of a formalized settlement, not evidence that family separation always improves performance.

**Politics to study.** Family identity, ownership, operational control, brand rights, succession, non-compete boundaries and the challenge of translating private family agreements into public-company disclosure and implementation.

**Skill lessons.** In a family-owned firm, identify which conflicts are about family recognition, ownership, control, money, legacy, or operating responsibilities. Separate the emotional relationship from the legal/business question. Document brand, board, transfer, conflict-resolution and succession arrangements before a crisis forces decisions.

**Source:** Godrej Industries’ stock-exchange filing / family settlement agreement: https://www.godrejindustries.com/uploads/GIL_Reg30_FSAF_8a798cfd04.pdf ; Reuters report: https://www.reuters.com/business/indias-godrej-family-agree-split-conglomerate-into-two-2024-05-01/

### Case 5 — Murugappa family dispute over board representation and succession (settled 2023)

**Public record.** Valli Arunachalam, daughter of late M. V. Murugappan, pursued a dispute seeking a board position at Ambadi Investments, the family’s holding company. Public reporting described her challenge to the group’s practice of excluding women family members from board roles. The family announced a settlement and withdrawal of legal proceedings in August 2023; the settlement terms were not publicly disclosed in the cited report.

**Politics to study.** Family membership, gender, governance rights, succession norms and the difference between a public narrative and a private settlement. A business can have sophisticated formal systems and still face contested informal assumptions about who is considered a successor.

**Skill lessons.** Succession criteria should be transparent, role-based and documented. Separate competence, ownership rights and family status. If bias is alleged, identify the specific rule, decision, evidence and remedy sought; do not infer undisclosed settlement terms.

**Source:** The New Indian Express, 21 August 2023: https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/karnataka/2023/Aug/21/murugappa-family-members-settle-dispute-withdraw-legal-proceedings-2607206.html

### Case 6 — Indian family-managed business HRM: owner values become people systems (research)

**Research record.** Research on Indian family-managed companies explains that the owner family’s culture can shape organizational culture, HR practices and corporate governance. A separate systematic review of family-business governance examines how family complexity can prompt formal family governance structures. These are research findings, not a claim that family management is automatically worse than professionally managed firms.

**Politics to study.** Recruitment through personal trust, informal pay decisions, relatives in key posts, founder overrides, inconsistent performance management and a mismatch between professionalization and family identity.

**Skill lessons.** Diagnose the actual company: does it have clear roles, written criteria, independent review and predictable exceptions? Do not label all relatives as unqualified or all long-serving employees as “yes-men.” Ask who owns the process, what standards are used, and whether the same rules apply to comparable employees.

**Sources:** Ganguly, “HR Dynamics in Family-managed Businesses in India” (2020): https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2631454119894742 ; Thakur & Sinha, systematic review of family governance (2024): https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00472778.2023.2284930

## B. Governance failures, fraud risks and control breakdowns

### Case 7 — Satyam: founder dominance, false reporting and board oversight (2008–2009)

**Public record.** After an attempted acquisition of Maytas companies triggered stakeholder opposition, Satyam’s chairman disclosed falsification of financial statements in January 2009. The US SEC later charged Satyam with overstating revenue, income and cash balances by more than US$1 billion over five years. Indian authorities investigated; the government suspended the old board and appointed directors to stabilize the company.

**Politics to study.** The difference between a persuasive executive narrative and independently verified evidence; related-party transactions; pressure to meet market expectations; weak challenge to a dominant founder; and the reputational cost of “not wanting to be the person who raises the problem.”

**Skill lessons.** Never substitute status, charisma or confidence for reconciliation of records. For a deal, ask who benefits, what independent valuation exists, which approvals are required, who has challenged assumptions, and whether the board has the complete evidence. Employees should raise a documented concern through the correct control function rather than joining rumor networks.

**Sources:** US SEC (5 April 2011): https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2011/2011-81.htm ; Government of India chronology: https://www.pib.gov.in/newsite/erelcontent.aspx?lang=2&reg=48&relid=56471

### Case 8 — IL&FS: board oversight and systemic interconnection (2018)

**Public record.** In October 2018 the Ministry of Corporate Affairs stated that it had moved the NCLT to supersede IL&FS’s existing board because it considered the group’s affairs prejudicial to public interest. The NCLT suspended the existing board and approved a new board. The case had consequences beyond a single company because the group was linked to lenders, investors, infrastructure projects and debt markets.

**Politics to study.** How risk can travel through interconnected entities, how formal governance can fail to detect or act on warning signs, and why “everyone else knew” is not an adequate control system.

**Skill lessons.** Map dependencies beyond the immediate team; surface liquidity, concentration and counterparty risks early; clarify who owns group-wide risk; and challenge the assumption that a prestigious board or brand guarantees effective oversight.

**Source:** Ministry of Corporate Affairs / Press Information Bureau, 1 October 2018: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1548136&lang=2&reg=48

### Case 9 — Yes Bank reconstruction (2020)

**Public record.** The Reserve Bank of India placed Yes Bank under a moratorium in March 2020 and published a reconstruction scheme after describing a rapidly deteriorating financial position and the absence of a credible capital-infusion plan. The RBI notice is the primary source for the regulatory intervention. Wider analyses discuss promoter and governance risk, but those interpretations should be attributed to their authors.

**Politics to study.** Concentration of decision-making, overconfidence around a successful leader, weak challenge, delayed escalation and the mismatch between public confidence and deteriorating underlying indicators.

**Skill lessons.** Use leading indicators and independent risk review rather than waiting for a visible crisis. A strong executive relationship should never replace documented approvals, independent challenge and transparent reporting. An employee escalating risk should describe observable indicators and decision consequences, not make unsupported accusations about a senior person’s motive.

**Source:** Reserve Bank of India, 6 March 2020: https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=49479

### Case 10 — Punjab National Bank / Nirav Modi–Mehul Choksi investigations (2018 onward)

**Public record.** The Ministry of Corporate Affairs announced that the government had ordered an SFIO investigation into 107 companies and seven LLPs connected with the Nirav Modi and Mehul Choksi groups in relation to the PNB fraud matter. A later government release describing a US bankruptcy examiner’s report discussed entities that allegedly created the appearance of independent companies and round-tripping among related entities. This should be read as an investigation and court-process record, not a blanket conclusion about everyone connected with those businesses.

**Politics to study.** Related-party networks can obscure beneficial control; exceptions can become normalized; employees may feel pressure to accept verbal assurances from powerful customers or senior managers; and internal controls can be defeated when approval and verification are not truly independent.

**Skill lessons.** Verify authority and approvals in the system of record, check conflicts and connected parties, reconcile independent evidence, preserve audit trails, and escalate unusual requests without alerting a subject in ways that could compromise an investigation.

**Sources:** MCA / PIB, 16 March 2018: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1524869&lang=2&reg=3 ; MCA / PIB recovery update: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1648579&lang=2&reg=3

### Case 11 — DHFL: alleged loan diversion and connected-party risk

**Public record.** A 2024 Delhi High Court order recounts the CBI charge-sheet case against DHFL’s former senior executives and others. As summarized in that order, the CBI alleged that a consortium of 17 banks sanctioned loans aggregating about ₹42,000 crore and that significant amounts were siphoned or misappropriated through falsified books. Those are allegations described in court proceedings; the cited order should not be represented as a final finding of guilt.

**Politics to study.** Connected entities, concentrated authority over loan flows, documentation complexity, audit trail gaps, and how the appearance of legitimate commercial transactions can make challenge difficult.

**Skill lessons.** Trace the source and destination of funds; distinguish company, promoter, affiliate and beneficial owner; check related-party approvals; and record who made each decision. For staff, never falsify a record or destroy information because “leadership has approved it verbally.”

**Source:** Delhi High Court order hosted by Indian Kanoon (31 May 2024): https://indiankanoon.org/doc/59068148/

## C. Employee politics, labour relations, workplace voice and power asymmetry

### Case 12 — Maruti Suzuki Manesar: industrial relations breakdown and catastrophic violence (2011–2012)

**Public record.** Tensions at Maruti Suzuki’s Manesar plant escalated around union formation, working conditions, disciplinary procedures and differences between permanent and contract workers. On 18 July 2012, violence at the plant led to the death of senior HR manager Awanish Kumar Dev and injuries to others. Academic case materials and worker-rights reports describe different emphases on the background and institutional context. The killing and attacks were grave acts; context is not justification for violence, and accounts should not be flattened into a single narrative.

**Politics to study.** Informal grievance accumulation, distrust of HR, perceived wage inequity, contract-labour stratification, union legitimacy, caste allegations reported around the incident, and the way an apparently small disciplinary event can activate a large backlog of unresolved issues.

**Skill lessons.** Treat repeated grievances as risk signals, not just “attitude problems.” Establish credible channels, neutral investigations, consistent discipline and meaningful worker representation. In an explosive conflict, prioritize safety, de-escalation, due process and qualified labour-relations expertise.

**Sources:** Oxford University Press teaching case (2022): https://academic.oup.com/book/44474/chapter-abstract/376429498 ; ICMR case overview: https://icmrindia.org/case-study-details?casecode=HROB160 ; worker-rights report (PUDR, 2013): https://www.pudr.org/publications/driving-force-labour-struggles-and-violation-of-rights-in-maruti-suzuki-india-limited/

### Case 13 — Foxconn Sriperumbudur: worker housing, supply chains and voice (2021–2022)

**Public record.** After more than 250 workers were reported sick following food poisoning at a hostel, workers protested and production at the Tamil Nadu plant was suspended for over three weeks. Reuters interviewed workers about crowded dormitories and poor facilities. Apple said some remote dormitory and dining facilities did not meet its requirements and that it was working with the supplier on corrective action. Those public findings connect workplace conditions beyond the factory gate to brand and supply-chain risk.

**Politics to study.** Contractors and suppliers can sit between a brand’s standards and a worker’s daily reality; workers may fear retaliation; managers may receive poor information from subcontractors; and reputational risk becomes operational risk.

**Skill lessons.** Include housing, transport, food, payroll and labour contractors in the stakeholder/system map. Do not confuse an audit certificate with continuous monitoring. Build confidential complaint routes, check whether remedy is implemented, and avoid punishing workers for raising safety concerns.

**Sources:** Reuters coverage syndicated by Euronews: https://www.euronews.com/next/2021/12/30/foxconn-india ; Reuters restart report syndicated by MarketScreener: https://in.marketscreener.com/news/latest/Foxconn-India-s-iPhone-plant-restarts-production-after-December-protests-37525853/

### Case 14 — Samsung Sriperumbudur strike: wages, hours and union dialogue (September 2024)

**Public record.** Hundreds of workers at Samsung’s Sriperumbudur plant began an indefinite strike seeking higher wages and improved working hours. Reuters reported partial production disruption and quoted both a union leader and the company’s response that it was engaging with workers and complying with law. This is a snapshot of the start of a dispute, not a full assessment of every subsequent negotiation or outcome.

**Politics to study.** Different definitions of fairness, productivity and recognition; the role of worker representation; production deadlines; the risk of using temporary/contract labour as a substitute for dialogue; and how both management and workers can become locked into public positions.

**Skill lessons.** Separate the substantive issues (pay, hours, conditions, recognition) from process issues (who speaks for workers, which forum is legitimate, how a proposal is evaluated). Use data on productivity, turnover, wages and working conditions; build a negotiation calendar; and make accurate public statements.

**Source:** Reuters, 9 September 2024: https://www.reuters.com/technology/workers-samsung-india-plant-strike-partly-hitting-production-2024-09-09/

### Case 15 — Wistron Narasapura: contractor payroll and escalation failure (2020)

**Public record.** Reuters reported that Wistron’s Karnataka facility was shut for two months after contract workers angered by unpaid wages damaged property and equipment in December 2020. The report sits within a broader pattern of labour-related disruption at manufacturing operations in India. Property damage should not be excused, but the pay and contractor control chain is relevant to risk prevention.

**Politics to study.** Employer–contractor distance, payroll transparency, grievance queues, delivery pressure and the failure of operational management to detect a growing wage problem before it becomes a crisis.

**Skill lessons.** Audit payroll timeliness and contractor accountability; offer a safe grievance escalation path; communicate pay calculations clearly; and investigate the actual cause before choosing discipline or remediation. The AI should not frame violence as a legitimate negotiation technique.

**Source:** Reuters summary of labour disruptions, 10 September 2024: https://www.reuters.com/world/india/labour-woes-india-operations-global-companies-2024-09-10/

### Case 16 — Tata Motors Sanand: suspension, strike and a negotiated settlement (2015–2016)

**Public record.** An open-access Springer chapter recounts that 26 workers were suspended after alleged misconduct involving vehicle damage. A sit-in strike followed, with workers demanding reinstatement and wage revision. After an extended meeting involving the company, workers’ representatives and the Labour Commissioner, the parties agreed to revoke 13 suspensions and review the remainder after inquiry, recognize the union and negotiate wages; legal questions remained for the appropriate forum.

**Politics to study.** Discipline versus due process, the timing and credibility of investigations, union recognition, wage bargaining and third-party mediation.

**Skill lessons.** Separate suspension pending inquiry from a final finding. Preserve evidence, define investigation timelines, keep the wage issue separate from allegations of misconduct when possible, and use mediation to reopen a blocked process. Written terms and named owners reduce later disputes.

**Source:** Springer chapter, “Mediation and Conciliation in Collective Labor Conflicts in India”: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-92531-8_18

### Case 17 — Bosch Jaipur: lockout, go-slow allegation and industrial relations (2015)

**Public record.** An Oxford University Press case study describes Bosch’s Jaipur plant lockout following management’s allegation of an illegal go-slow action and an indefinite hunger strike by union office bearers. The abstract frames the case around MNC culture and industrial relations; a short abstract is not enough to determine the merits of each side’s position.

**Politics to study.** Cultural differences in management expectations, productivity-linked pay, union-management trust and the difference between a party’s allegation and an adjudicated fact.

**Skill lessons.** Establish measurable productivity definitions, a legitimate process for disputed output, clear rules for lockouts/strikes and a third-party route that both sides accept. Avoid interpreting collective worker action simply as disloyalty or management discipline simply as retaliation without evidence.

**Source:** Oxford University Press teaching-case abstract: https://academic.oup.com/book/44469/chapter-abstract/392562086

### Case 18 — Wipro versus former CFO Jatin Dalal: exit restrictions and employee mobility (2023–2024)

**Public record.** Wipro sued its former CFO after he joined Cognizant, alleging breach of a post-employment non-compete and seeking damages. In January 2024, a Bengaluru civil court referred the dispute to arbitration under the parties’ agreements. Referral to arbitration is a procedural decision; it was not a final ruling that the clause was enforceable or that the former executive was liable.

**Politics to study.** Loyalty narratives after departure, confidentiality, trade secrets, competition and the difference between a contract clause being written and being legally enforceable.

**Skill lessons.** Before leaving, read the actual contract, maintain confidentiality, preserve company property and return access credentials; do not take client lists or proprietary material. Employers should use proportionate protections and current legal advice rather than assuming every non-compete clause will be enforced. Indian law is especially fact-sensitive; the AI must not give categorical legal conclusions without current legal research.

**Sources:** The Indian Express, 3 January 2024: https://indianexpress.com/article/cities/bangalore/wipros-case-ex-cfo-jatin-dalal-non-compete-clause-referred-arbitration-9094003/lite/ ; India employment-law overview (2026): https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/employment-2026/india

## D. Academic teaching cases and research on everyday employee politics

These are useful because much everyday workplace politics is not litigated or reported under the employer’s real name. They should not be presented as publicly verified scandals involving a named corporation.

### Case 19 — “Advanced IT Systems Limited”: conflict of interest in a promotion recommendation

A published teaching case describes a family-owned Mumbai software company in which a business head advocates for a junior employee’s fast-track promotion while failing to disclose a personal relationship with her. The learning issue is undisclosed conflict of interest, not whether the candidate is competent.

**Skill lessons:** disclose conflicts; use independent criteria and reviewers; separate personal relationships from performance evidence; protect privacy; do not punish the employee merely because a manager had a conflict.

**Source:** Patanjali & Gupta, Asian Journal of Management Cases (2020): https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0972820119892703

### Case 20 — “Precision Engineering”: formalizing HR in a family-centric business

An academic case describes a family-run automotive-component manufacturer that wants to grow while relying on informal hiring, training and performance-management practices. It focuses on how HR can professionalize the system without destroying useful employee-centred traditions.

**Skill lessons:** define job criteria, structured interviews, training plans, performance standards, documented exceptions and a path for employees to challenge inconsistent decisions. Informality can support speed and trust, but becomes risky when employees cannot predict how decisions are made.

**Source:** Emerald Emerging Markets Case Studies: https://www.emerald.com/eemcs/article-abstract/doi/10.1108/EEMCS-03-2022-0051/460872/Formalising-human-resource-practices-in-a-family?redirectedFrom=fulltext

### Case 21 — “Durga and Company”: succession conflict and gender bias in a closely held business

A published teaching case analyses conflict in a closely held family business, including succession planning and the risk that women family members are discounted without a transparent competency-based process. Treat it as an instructional case, not a public allegation against a named firm.

**Skill lessons:** distinguish inheritance rights, board appointment criteria, family relationships and operating competence. Plan succession before a founder exits; set dispute prevention, management and resolution processes.

**Source:** Joshi et al., “Conflicts in a Closely Held Family Business: Durga and Company” (2018): https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0972262917750653

### Research note — employee silence, power and credit

An Academy of Management study using a sample of 781 employees across multiple Indian firms found that upward communication was associated with how employees perceived supervisor power and organizational politics, and with their own political skill. A separate study of employees from India, the United States and China reported that some Indian participants described lack of acknowledgment, including credit being taken for their work, as a trigger for trust violation. These findings help generate hypotheses; they do not establish how any one Indian colleague will behave.

**Skill lessons:** upward communication should match the decision-maker’s legitimate information needs; contribution records should be factual and collaborative; a failure to acknowledge work should be tested with specific examples before inferring intent.

**Sources:** Kumar & Mishra, Academy of Management Proceedings: https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/ambpp.2015.13633abstract ; Wildman et al. (2022): https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14705958221112755

## E. Cross-case pattern library: everyday politics to recognize

These are diagnostic patterns, not claims about a specific firm. For every pattern, the AI must ask what evidence would confirm or disconfirm it.

1. **Owner bottleneck:** all meaningful decisions wait for the owner, so titles and procedures may be weaker than direct access. Response: confirm delegated limits, send concise option-based decisions, and avoid promising timelines without owner approval.
2. **Inner-circle information advantage:** a small group hears about changes before others. Response: build legitimate relationships across functions, ask for the official decision process, and avoid gossip or unauthorized information gathering.
3. **Founder shadow:** the founder is no longer in the operating role but retains veto or symbolic authority. Response: clarify mandate, escalation and public/private decision rights early.
4. **Old guard versus professional managers:** legacy employees value trust and tacit knowledge; new leaders value metrics and process. Response: respect institutional knowledge, make process improvements jointly, and demonstrate how standards protect continuity rather than insult loyalty.
5. **Nepotism/favouritism suspicion:** a relative or close associate receives access, resources or promotion. Response: compare documented criteria, scope and results; do not assume incompetence from kinship alone.
6. **Credit capture:** someone presents a shared result as their own. Response: record contributions in project updates, use shared authorship, and correct the record neutrally with specific facts.
7. **Blame shifting:** an owner of a decision reframes it as someone else’s execution failure. Response: send decision summaries with owners, assumptions, dependencies and risks before work starts.
8. **Gatekeeping:** someone controls meetings, data, customer contact or senior access. Response: make dependencies visible, establish a shared source of truth, and request an explicit service/decision SLA.
9. **Verbal order / written denial:** a senior person gives an informal instruction then disclaims it. Response: send a neutral written recap; for unsafe, unlawful or fraudulent directions, do not seek a cosmetic confirmation—stop and use the right control channel.
10. **Divide-and-rule / triangulation:** colleagues receive different messages that make them distrust each other. Response: confirm decisions directly with relevant owners, avoid forwarding gossip, and align on a written shared brief.
11. **Performance-review ambush:** concerns appear for the first time during appraisal or a PIP. Response: ask for examples, measurable expectations, baseline, review dates, support and appeal process; maintain factual records.
12. **Promotion opacity:** criteria are vague or change late. Response: request written role expectations, competencies, evidence and decision timeline; prepare a portfolio mapped to the criteria.
13. **Resource politics:** projects are prioritized through executive attention rather than value alone. Response: compare options against shared criteria (revenue, customer risk, compliance, strategic fit, capacity) and show opportunity costs.
14. **Meeting theatre:** a decision is effectively made before the meeting. Response: learn the legitimate pre-read and consultation process, submit concise evidence early, and do not fabricate consensus.
15. **Silent veto:** stakeholders delay without saying no. Response: ask what evidence or condition is missing, set a decision date, and identify whether the blocker is capacity, risk, authority or disagreement.
16. **Scapegoating after failure:** the person with least power receives blame. Response: reconstruct a timeline using contemporaneous records, examine decision rights and dependencies, and raise systemic causes without hiding personal accountability.
17. **Contract-versus-permanent divide:** different groups perform related work under different pay, benefits, security or representation arrangements. Response: map both formal terms and practical impact; do not assume every disparity is lawful or unlawful without specific facts and current local advice.
18. **Union or collective voice treated as individual disloyalty:** the organization sees collective grievances as a discipline issue only. Response: distinguish individual misconduct from collective bargaining, use recognized forums and current labour rules.
19. **HR as management proxy:** employees distrust HR because they see it as protecting leadership rather than applying a fair process. Response: ask who owns the investigation, whether the investigator is independent, how confidentiality and anti-retaliation are handled, and what review mechanism exists.
20. **Whistleblower as enemy:** raising concerns is framed as betrayal. Response: preserve evidence and use a protected channel; no retaliation, threats, doxxing or campaign to identify an anonymous reporter.
21. **Ethical fading:** questionable action is relabelled as “business urgency,” “industry practice,” “owner’s instruction” or “temporary adjustment.” Response: name the actual decision and affected parties, check policy/law, and ask for an approved compliant alternative.
22. **Supplier/agent intermediary opacity:** a vendor, staffing company or agent is blamed for what the principal organization benefits from. Response: map who sets terms, who pays, who monitors and who can remedy the harm.
23. **Exit politics:** an employee’s resignation triggers allegations about loyalty, access, clients or confidential data. Response: follow documented offboarding, return assets, preserve personal copies only where lawful, and avoid taking confidential files.
24. **Acquisition/restructuring turf war:** teams compete to preserve budget, reporting lines, status and headcount. Response: establish decision principles, criteria, consultation windows and transition owners.
25. **Public-narrative contest:** leaders use selective facts to win stakeholder sympathy. Response: anchor analysis in dated primary records, identify each side’s claims, and label what remains unresolved.

## F. Case-study method for an AI assistant

For any new real-world case, generate:

1. **Case identity:** organization, country, period, industry and decision arena.
2. **Source-quality map:** primary record (court/regulator/company filing), reputable journalism, academic analysis, advocacy/worker account, and unverified commentary. Do not use anonymous social posts as core facts.
3. **Timeline:** 5–12 dated events, with sources beside each material claim.
4. **Actors and power:** formal authority, ownership, budget, expertise, information, implementation control, worker/customer impact and legitimate channels.
5. **Competing explanations:** at least two where motives are uncertain. Explicitly distinguish evidence, allegation, finding and interpretation.
6. **Mechanism:** why this political dynamic mattered—decision rights, incentive, dependency, information asymmetry, trust, culture, process failure or governance gap.
7. **Tactical choices:** what each actor could realistically do, with consequences and trade-offs; never assume perfect information.
8. **Counterfactual prevention:** what early signals could have been noticed and what safer process could have changed the path.
9. **Transferability:** which lessons generalize and which are specific to law, industry, scale, geography or governance form.
10. **Skill drill:** a scenario, stakeholder map, draft message, ethical checkpoint and model answer.

### Evidence labels

- **Verified public record:** supported by a primary source or clearly documented event.
- **Reported claim:** a statement attributed to a person, party or news outlet.
- **Regulatory/court allegation:** formal allegation not equivalent to final finding.
- **Finding/order:** actual conclusion in a regulator or court document; capture scope precisely.
- **Academic interpretation:** analysis by researchers, not an adjudicated fact.
- **Hypothesis:** a plausible explanation that remains to be tested.

### Research limitations

This is a curated starter casebook, not a complete review of Indian corporate history. Some full academic cases are paywalled; their public abstracts are used only for the issues the abstract supports. Court and regulator records can be lengthy, and a future AI answer should open the original record before making a high-stakes claim. For current employment rules, verify Central and relevant State notifications rather than relying on a static pack.
