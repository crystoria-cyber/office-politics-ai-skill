# Corporate Political Skills — Practical Playbook

This file expands the core `SKILL.md`. Treat each framework as a decision aid, not a universal law. Validate it against the organization's culture, rules, country, and facts.

## 1. Diagnose the decision, not just the people
Before trying to persuade anyone, answer:
- What decision is actually needed, and what is outside the decision's scope?
- Who formally owns it? Who controls budget, headcount, risk approval, customer access, implementation, or the calendar?
- What criteria will be used: revenue, risk, cost, strategic fit, capacity, customer impact, policy, or political acceptability?
- At what stage can the decision still change? Which concerns can be resolved before the formal meeting?
- What happens if nothing changes?

Often a so-called political blocker is responding to a genuine constraint: an unowned cost, risk, workload, conflicting KPI, reputational exposure, or lack of evidence. Test these explanations first.

## 2. Stakeholder map
For each stakeholder, record:
- Role / relationship to the decision
- Formal authority (decision, approval, veto, budget, delivery)
- Informal influence (expertise, trust, network reach, credibility)
- Interest / outcome they likely care about
- Concern, cost, risk, or constraint they may see
- Evidence of current stance; never mark a stance based only on rumor
- What they need to know, decide, or contribute
- Best engagement method and timing
- Confidence (high / medium / low) and how to validate

Use a power/interest grid as a first pass:
- High power / high interest: engage directly and regularly.
- High power / low interest: provide concise risk and decision updates; avoid flooding them.
- Low power / high interest: consult genuinely; these people may hold execution knowledge or bear the impact.
- Low power / low interest: provide proportionate updates when relevant.

Add two checks: (1) who can delay implementation without formal veto, and (2) who has legitimate expertise or bears consequences despite low power. Revisit the map when facts change.

## 3. Infer interests without mind-reading
Use a hypothesis table:

| Observable evidence | Possible explanation A | Possible explanation B | Low-risk test |
|---|---|---|---|
| A director asks repeatedly about staffing | They believe capacity is the main risk | They doubt the forecast or ownership model | Ask what evidence would make capacity acceptable |
| A peer avoids committing in a meeting | They lack authority to commit | They disagree but do not want public conflict | Ask privately what decision or input they need |
| A sponsor delays the proposal | Timing or competing priorities | They see an unresolved risk | Ask whether the issue is timing, evidence, sponsorship, or something else |

Do not label people “jealous,” “narcissistic,” “toxic,” or “against me” based on one event. Describe the behavior, list plausible explanations, and choose a test that does not create unnecessary risk.

## 4. Influence without authority: six-step sequence
1. **Understand** — ask about the other team's priorities, constraints, definitions of success, and non-negotiables.
2. **Connect** — explain the shared outcome and the specific problem being solved.
3. **Prove** — bring evidence, customer impact, options, comparable cases, workload estimates, or a small demonstration.
4. **Reduce risk** — propose a pilot, staged approval, rollback plan, clear owner, or review date.
5. **Invite shaping** — ask what would make the proposal more workable and incorporate useful input.
6. **Agree a small next action** — secure a named owner and date, rather than relying on vague goodwill.

A good influence request gives the other person a legitimate reason to support it. Do not frame every request as “what is in it for you”; value can include mission, quality, reduced risk, learning, fairness, customer outcomes, or lower workload.

## 5. Build coalitions, not factions
Healthy coalition-building has four characteristics: shared purpose, informed consent, fair contribution, and accurate representation of support. Brief relevant stakeholders before a decision meeting so they can surface objections and improve the proposal—not to ambush dissenters or manufacture the impression of consensus.

Practical methods:
- Ask a respected expert to review the evidence, not merely endorse you.
- Invite affected teams to co-design implementation.
- Credit contributors explicitly.
- Share decision criteria and unresolved trade-offs.
- Record where agreement exists and where it does not.

Avoid secret pacts against a colleague, selective disclosure designed to mislead, or saying “everyone supports this” unless that is true and relevant.

## 6. Executive communication: decision-ready brief
Use this structure for leadership updates:
1. **Recommendation:** one sentence.
2. **Decision needed:** approve / choose / unblock / advise; include the date.
3. **Why now:** business objective, customer impact, timing, or risk.
4. **Evidence:** 2–4 high-value facts, with sources or assumptions noted.
5. **Options and trade-offs:** at least the recommended option and one credible alternative.
6. **Risks and mitigations:** name material risks honestly.
7. **Ask and ownership:** resources, decision, owner, and next review.

For difficult news, use: **what happened → impact → what is known/unknown → options → recommendation → help/decision needed**. Do not bury material risk in an appendix or surprise a leader with avoidable bad news at the last moment.

## 7. Negotiation preparation
Prepare these items before a high-stakes negotiation:
- Desired outcome and ranked interests (what matters, and why)
- Other party's possible interests and constraints, marked as hypotheses
- BATNA: your best realistic alternative if no agreement is reached
- Steps to improve the BATNA and realistic limits / reservation point
- Possible agreement options and trades across issues
- Objective criteria for a fair decision
- Relationship importance and future interactions
- Your authority to commit and topics requiring approval
- Communication plan, participants, sequence, agenda, and timing
- Ethical pitfalls and potential harm to people not in the room

Do not use a guessed BATNA as a fact. Do not make false claims about competing offers, deadlines, policy, executive backing, or what others have agreed to. A negotiated agreement is not automatically good: compare it with the alternative and implementation risks.

## 8. Reputation, visibility, credit, and promotion
The objective is to make impact legible, not to exaggerate personal credit.
- Maintain a concise evidence log: project, baseline, action, result, collaborators, and proof.
- Agree success measures and review dates before delivering work.
- Give credit in public and resolve material misattribution with specific evidence in the smallest appropriate forum first, if safe.
- Ask a manager: “What evidence would you need to see to rate me as ready for the next level?”
- Request sponsorship as a concrete action: feedback on a proposal, introduction to the decision owner, a chance to present results, or advocacy based on documented work.
- Build relationships beyond the immediate manager through useful collaboration, knowledge sharing, mentoring, and reliable follow-through.

Avoid constant self-promotion, inflated attribution, or making colleagues look bad to seem stronger.

## 9. Difficult conversations
A practical script:
- **Observation:** “In the last two project reviews, the launch date was communicated before our team confirmed readiness.”
- **Impact:** “That created conflicting expectations for the client and delivery team.”
- **Perspective:** “Can you help me understand what drove the timing?”
- **Need / boundary:** “We need delivery to confirm readiness before a date is shared externally.”
- **Request:** “Can we agree that future dates are marked provisional until both teams sign off?”
- **Follow-up:** “I’ll send the proposed checklist today and review it with you on Friday.”

Use concrete observations, avoid accusations about motive, and allow for correction. If the matter involves discrimination, harassment, fraud, safety, serious misconduct, or retaliation, consider whether a direct conversation could increase risk; use an appropriate formal channel instead.

## 10. Responding to obstruction, exclusion, or credit-taking
1. Write a factual timeline with dates, documents, decisions, and witnesses where relevant.
2. Separate direct evidence from interpretation. Preserve only records you are authorized to access and retain.
3. Determine the needed outcome: correction, decision clarity, access to information, workload fix, protection from retaliation, or formal review.
4. Where safe, ask a focused, neutral question or confirm the process in writing.
5. Seek a trusted adviser or neutral manager if the pattern continues.
6. Escalate serious or repeated conduct through the appropriate HR, compliance, ombuds, employee representative, or legal channel.
7. Avoid retaliation, public speculation, unauthorized recording, or counter-gossip.

## 11. Change-management politics
When a proposal meets resistance, explore:
- Who loses control, budget, status, expertise, autonomy, predictability, or resources?
- Who inherits work or risk that the proposal does not make visible?
- Which incentives currently reward the status quo?
- Which people have implementation knowledge that was not consulted?
- Can a pilot, transition period, training, clear metric, or shared governance reduce legitimate concerns?
- How will the decision be reviewed if assumptions prove wrong?

Resistance can be data. Do not assume it must be overcome; sometimes the right answer is redesigning the proposal.

## 12. Ethical influence check
Before sending a message or making a move, ask:
- Is every factual claim accurate and reasonably supportable?
- Have I represented other people's views and endorsements truthfully?
- Does the other party have enough information to make a meaningful decision?
- Am I using a legitimate process and respecting confidentiality?
- Who could be harmed or unfairly burdened?
- Would I be willing to explain this strategy to a neutral reviewer?
- Is there a lower-pressure option that could still work?

If the answer creates concern, pause and choose a more transparent method.

## 13. Risk ladder for escalation
Use proportionate channels; follow applicable policy and local law.
- **Level 1 — Clarify:** ask a specific question or correct a misunderstanding.
- **Level 2 — Align:** agree roles, criteria, ownership, and written follow-up with the relevant people.
- **Level 3 — Facilitate:** involve a neutral manager, project lead, mediator, or ombuds function.
- **Level 4 — Formal process:** HR, compliance, ethics, whistleblowing, safety, union/employee representation, or legal advice as appropriate.

Serious safety threats, fraud, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation can warrant formal escalation immediately; do not require the user to attempt direct confrontation first.

## 14. Measure success
Use both outcome and relationship measures:
- Was the decision made by the needed date?
- Were decision criteria and ownership clear?
- Did implementation risk, rework, or delay decrease?
- Did affected stakeholders understand the trade-offs?
- Were commitments met and credit attributed accurately?
- Did the process maintain space for dissent and risk reporting?
- What new evidence changed the stakeholder map?

Do not measure success only by “getting my way.” A robust agreement that is implemented fairly may be better than a nominal win that destroys trust or creates hidden costs.

## 14. India: owner-led / family-managed firm diagnostic

Use the details in `INDIA_CONTEXT.md`. Start with evidence instead of a label. For a real workplace, create separate boxes for the owner/family, formal leadership, long-serving insiders, new professional managers, HR/finance/control functions, contractors and affected employees. A person can occupy more than one role; record the role relevant to this decision.

Ask six questions:
1. Which decisions are formally delegated, and which are usually pulled back to the owner?
2. Who can access the owner and influence priority, and is that access legitimate for the decision?
3. Which people systems are written (role scope, salary bands, appraisal, promotion, grievance) and which are discretionary?
4. How are exceptions recorded, explained and applied to comparable employees?
5. Can a staff member surface a risk without humiliation or retaliation?
6. What is the plan for succession, continuity and family/management disagreement?

**Practical intervention order:** clarify one decision right; document one recurring exception; improve one feedback loop; pilot a simple HR standard; only then propose larger process redesign. The aim is to create predictability without insulting the founder or erasing useful tacit knowledge.

## 15. Labour-relations and collective voice

When a conflict involves a union, plant, contractors or collective grievance, do not treat it as ordinary interpersonal politics. Map:
- What is the documented grievance and what is alleged?
- Which groups are affected (permanent, contract, apprentice, supervisors, contractors, women workers, hostel residents, etc.)?
- Which employer or contractor controls pay, working hours, safety, transport, food, housing and discipline?
- What collective representation and formal dialogue routes exist?
- What rules apply now in the relevant State and establishment?
- What is the safe de-escalation plan and which issues require investigation or formal process?

Separate the right to raise a grievance from any separate misconduct allegation. An incident can involve legitimate grievances and unacceptable individual acts at the same time. Do not romanticize violence, suppress worker voice, or assume the company's version or the union's version is automatically complete.

## 16. Boardroom and financial-governance cases

For board, promoter and financial-control disputes, focus on: who had decision authority; what was disclosed; whether conflicts of interest were recorded; how independent challenge worked; what records were available; whether related-party or connected-entity risk existed; and exactly what the court/regulator decided. Do not frame serious financial misconduct as merely clever politics. Fraud, falsification and control evasion are not acceptable influence techniques.
