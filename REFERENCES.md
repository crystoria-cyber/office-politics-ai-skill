# Research and Reference Library

**Web research reviewed through 27 October 2026.** This is a curated, practical bibliography, not a claim to have exhaustively reviewed every publication on organizational politics. Links and publication dates can change. Use current official sources for jurisdiction-specific questions.

## A. Foundational research on political skill

1. **Ferris, G. R., et al. (2007). “Political Skill in Organizations.” _Journal of Management_, 33(3).**
   - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206307300813
   - Why it matters: Foundational theory describing political skill as a workplace social-effectiveness construct and situating it within cognition, affect, and behavior.

2. **Jacobson, R. K., & Viswesvaran, C. (2017). “A Reliability Generalization Study of the Political Skill Inventory.” _SAGE Open_.**
   - https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244017706714
   - Why it matters: Reviews reliability evidence for the Political Skill Inventory (PSI). The article reports a weighted mean reliability estimate of .89 for the 18-item PSI across 77 samples; this is measurement evidence, not proof that every tactic works in every workplace.

3. **Munyon, T. P., Summers, J. K., Thompson, K. M., & Ferris, G. R. (2015). “Political Skill and Work Outcomes: A Theoretical Extension, Meta-Analytic Investigation, and Agenda for the Future.” _Personnel Psychology_, 68(1), 143–184.**
   - https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/peps.12066
   - Why it matters: Meta-analysis found political skill was positively related to several outcomes such as job satisfaction, work productivity, organizational commitment, career success, and reputation, and negatively related to physiological strain. Associations are not guarantees of causation or universal success.

4. **Jacobson & Viswesvaran (2017), PSI reliability study — accessible entry.**
   - https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244017706714
   - Four commonly used dimensions in the literature are social astuteness, interpersonal influence, networking ability, and apparent sincerity. This skill treats sincerity as actual trustworthiness, not merely an appearance to perform.

5. **Quality Improvement Center for Workforce Development (2022). “Political Skill.”**
   - https://qic-wd.org/umbrella-summary/political-skill
   - Why it matters: Accessible synthesis describing the four dimensions and distinguishing constructive political skill from harmful or self-serving organizational politics.

6. **Chang, C.-H., Rosen, C. C., & Levy, P. E. (2009). “The Relationship Between Perceptions of Organizational Politics and Employee Attitudes, Strain, and Behavior: A Meta-Analytic Examination.” _Academy of Management Journal_.**
   - https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amj.2009.43670894
   - Why it matters: Meta-analytic evidence linked perceived organizational politics with strain, turnover intentions, lower job satisfaction, lower affective commitment, and lower task performance. It helps explain why an organization should not normalize destructive political behavior.

7. **“Political skill and self-serving counterproductive work behaviors: Moderating role of perceptions of organizational politics.” _Journal of Management & Organization_ (2022 issue).**
   - https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-management-and-organization/article/abs/political-skill-and-selfserving-counterproductive-work-behaviors-moderating-role-of-perceptions-of-organizational-politics/FFC86CBFA7FC46354372DE6025104A38
   - Why it matters: Highlights a “dark side”: political skill can be used for self-serving counterproductive behavior in environments where politics are perceived to be high. Skill and ethics must be considered separately.

8. **“The job crafting paradox: How political skill shapes employee engagement and exhaustion.” _Organizational Dynamics_ (September 2026).**
   - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0090261626000380
   - Why it matters: A recent study based on two time-lagged studies of more than 550 US employees examines how political skill shapes the consequences of job crafting. Treat findings as context-specific emerging evidence, not a guarantee.

## B. Stakeholder mapping and informal networks

9. **UK Office for National Statistics / Government Analysis Function (published 2 February 2024). “Stakeholder mapping.”**
   - https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/stakeholder-mapping/
   - Why it matters: Practical guidance to map stakeholders by power/influence and interest, then set engagement levels. This is a useful starting matrix; CPS adds affected people, evidence quality, formal decision rights, and dynamic relationships.

10. **UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (published 16 April 2026). “Understanding stakeholders and political networks: practical guidance note.”**
    - https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/understanding-stakeholders-and-political-networks-practical-guidance-note
    - Why it matters: Recent guidance distinguishes stakeholder analysis from political-network mapping and encourages evidence-based analysis of relationships. Its original context is international cooperation and development; apply the general method thoughtfully rather than assuming the context is identical to a corporation.

11. **McKinsey (1 November 2007). “Harnessing the power of informal employee networks.”**
    - https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/harnessing-the-power-of-informal-employee-networks
    - Why it matters: Explains how informal peer networks can support collaboration and knowledge flow alongside formal structures. It is a practitioner article and older, so use it as supporting context rather than current empirical proof.

12. **Bradford, D. L., & Cohen, A. R. _Influence Without Authority_ (2nd ed.; Stanford GSB book page).**
    - https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/books/influence-without-authority-2nd-edition
    - Why it matters: A widely used management framework for gaining cooperation across authority boundaries through reciprocal value and work relationships.

## C. Negotiation and persuasion

13. **Harvard Law School Program on Negotiation. “A Negotiation Preparation Checklist.”**
    - https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/negotiation-preparation-checklist/
    - Why it matters: Structured preparation covering goals, strengths, interests, BATNA, reservation point, other-party interests, objective benchmarks, authority, and ethics.

14. **Harvard Law School Program on Negotiation. “What Is Negotiation? Understanding the Seven Elements of Successful Negotiation.”**
    - https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/what-is-negotiation/
    - Why it matters: Summarizes a preparation framework around interests, alternatives, options, legitimacy, relationships, commitments, and communication.

15. **Harvard Law School Program on Negotiation. “Principled Negotiation: Focus on Interests to Create Value.” (30 June 2026).**
    - https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/principled-negotiation-focus-interests-create-value/
    - Why it matters: Recent practitioner explanation of focusing on underlying interests, generating options, using objective criteria, and comparing an agreement with BATNA.

16. **Harvard Law School Program on Negotiation. “Top 10 Negotiation Skills You Must Learn to Succeed.” (6 October 2026).**
    - https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/top-10-negotiation-skills/
    - Why it matters: Recent overview of integrative negotiation skills and balancing value creation with protection of legitimate interests.

17. **Robert Cialdini. “The Seven Principles of Persuasion.”**
    - https://cialdini.com/principles
    - Why it matters: Summarizes reciprocity, scarcity, authority, commitment/consistency, liking, social proof, and unity. CPS applies persuasion only where truthful, proportionate, and non-coercive; scarcity and authority claims must never be fabricated.

18. **Harvard Business Review (11 August 2026). “Wielding Influence When You Lack Authority.”**
    - https://hbr.org/tip/2026/08/wielding-influence-when-you-lack-authority
    - Why it matters: Recent practitioner guidance on influencing peers and leaders without relying on a title. As a short practitioner tip, use alongside the broader research and not as a stand-alone empirical source.

## D. Psychological safety and team learning

19. **Edmondson, A. C. (1999). “Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams.” _Administrative Science Quarterly_, 44(2), 350–383.**
    - https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/13a7b031-0fdd-45ec-a7e0-2b80e2bc679f
    - DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999
    - Why it matters: Study of 51 work teams found psychological safety was associated with team learning behavior, which mediated team performance in the studied setting. It supports surfacing risks and dissent rather than silencing people.

20. **Amy C. Edmondson, “Psychological Safety.”**
    - https://amycedmondson.com/psychological-safety/
    - Why it matters: Researcher-authored summary of psychological safety as a condition that supports speaking up, learning, and collaboration.

## How to use this library
- Use the foundational research to understand the construct and its limits.
- Use public guidance to structure stakeholder analysis, but adapt it to the organization's context.
- Use negotiation frameworks to prepare, not to script people into compliance.
- Use persuasion principles as truthful framing aids, not as tricks.
- Use psychological-safety research to protect the organization's ability to surface inconvenient facts.
- For high-stakes decisions, read original material rather than relying solely on summaries. This list is curated, not exhaustive.
