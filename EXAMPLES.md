# Corporate Political Skills — Worked Examples

These examples are fictional. They demonstrate the reasoning pattern; they are not universal scripts.

## Example 1: A cross-functional project is being blocked

**User situation:** “Marketing wants to launch next month, but operations keeps raising objections. How do I get operations out of the way?”

**CPS diagnosis:** “Get them out of the way” assumes the objections are illegitimate. Possible causes include capacity, support readiness, quality, compliance, or a different incentive structure. The decision owner and launch criteria are not yet clear.

**Facts / hypotheses / unknowns:**
- Fact: Operations has raised concerns about the launch date.
- Hypotheses: They lack capacity; they believe quality risk is too high; they were consulted too late; or they disagree with the forecast.
- Unknowns: Specific objections, who owns go/no-go, what evidence is required, and what changes could make the launch safe.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Ask operations to rank their top two launch risks and state what evidence would resolve each.
2. Map the launch decision owner, operational sign-off, and teams that will inherit the work.
3. Build three options: full launch on the proposed date; limited pilot; or phased launch after specific readiness criteria.
4. Compare options against shared criteria—customer value, operational capacity, risk, cost, and reversibility.
5. Ask for a joint decision meeting, share a written decision brief, and record the agreed owner, criteria, and date.

**Suggested message:** “I want to find a launch plan that meets the customer goal without creating unmanageable operational risk. Could we list the two or three conditions that must be true for Operations to support a launch, then compare a full launch with a limited pilot against those conditions?”

**Why this fits CPS:** It turns resistance into testable information, gives affected teams a voice, and provides a legitimate route to a decision without personalizing the disagreement.

## Example 2: A senior leader appears to ignore your proposal

**User situation:** “My VP hasn't responded to my proposal for two weeks. They are probably trying to block me.”

**CPS diagnosis:** Silence is ambiguous. The VP might be overloaded, waiting for budget information, unclear on the ask, or unconvinced. Do not treat the motive as established.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Re-send a short decision-ready summary, not the full proposal alone.
2. Name the decision requested and the latest useful date.
3. Ask whether the issue is timing, evidence, risk, scope, or priority.
4. If there is no response, use the team's normal prioritization route or ask the manager how to sequence competing work—do not build a secret campaign around a guessed motive.

**Suggested follow-up:** “I’m following up on the proposal below. The decision needed is whether we should run the two-team pilot in November. The main upside is [evidence]; the main trade-off is [risk/cost]. Would you prefer that I (A) refine the business case, (B) reduce the scope, or (C) revisit this in the next planning cycle? A quick steer by [date] would help us plan capacity.”

## Example 3: A colleague presents your work as their own

**User situation:** “A colleague took credit for my work in a leadership meeting. Help me expose them.”

**CPS diagnosis:** The impact may be real, but public accusations can create avoidable risk. First establish exactly what was said, what record exists, and what correction is needed.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Preserve ordinary work artifacts you are authorized to access: dated drafts, project logs, decision notes, and task assignments.
2. Identify the specific misattribution and the correction needed.
3. If safe and proportionate, speak privately: “In yesterday's review, the analysis was described as your team's work. I produced the model and draft with input from [people]. Can we correct the record in the project recap and clarify ownership in the next update?”
4. If it recurs, keep a factual pattern log and seek a manager or formal channel. Avoid counter-gossip or exaggeration.

**Why this fits CPS:** It protects factual accuracy and the user's contribution while preserving proportionality and a path to resolution.

## Example 4: You need a promotion and have limited visibility

**User situation:** “I deliver results but senior leaders don't know me. How do I get politically powerful?”

**CPS diagnosis:** The likely gap may be visibility, unclear readiness criteria, missing sponsorship, or results that are not connected to business outcomes. “Powerful” is too vague to act on.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Ask the manager for the next-level criteria and the evidence that would demonstrate readiness.
2. Build a concise impact log: baseline, action, result, collaborators, and proof.
3. Seek assignments that expose the user to relevant cross-functional problems and decision-makers while producing real value.
4. Share short progress notes tied to agreed outcomes; credit collaborators.
5. Ask a mentor/sponsor for specific support: review a proposal, provide feedback, or create an opportunity to present work.
6. Review evidence against criteria on a date agreed in advance.

**Suggested question:** “I’d like to prepare for the next level deliberately. What two or three pieces of evidence would most clearly demonstrate readiness, and what project or responsibility would let me build that evidence over the next quarter?”

## Example 5: A team uses pressure and rumors to secure resources

**User situation:** “Other teams exaggerate deadlines and claim the CEO supports them. Should I do the same?”

**CPS diagnosis:** Copying dishonest tactics could undermine credibility, violate policy, and create a fragile commitment. The user can compete more effectively with verifiable evidence and clearer decision criteria.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Identify the actual resource decision and who owns it.
2. Build a comparable request: expected value, cost of delay, dependencies, risk, options, and opportunity cost.
3. Ask leadership to apply shared prioritization criteria across requests.
4. If someone states executive support, verify through a legitimate channel instead of repeating or spreading the claim.
5. If the practice indicates serious misconduct, document facts and use an appropriate formal channel.

**Suggested message:** “To make prioritization fair across teams, could we agree on common criteria for urgency, business impact, risk, and capacity? I can submit our request against those criteria and note what we would defer if it is not prioritized.”

## Example 6: The “Lala company” owner bottleneck

**User situation:** “I work at a family-owned manufacturer. My manager approves a purchase, but the owner later reverses it. Everyone says not to question the owner. How should I handle it?”

**CPS diagnosis:** The description suggests a possible gap between formal delegation and actual approval practice. It does not prove bad faith. The owner may be protecting cash flow, avoiding a risk, or centralizing decisions out of habit. The manager may have misunderstood their approval limit.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Check the written approval matrix, purchase policy, budget and previous comparable decisions.
2. Ask the manager how the owner wants exceptions presented and whether there is a threshold for delegated spending.
3. Prepare a one-page decision note: purpose, supplier options, cash timing, risk, cost of delay and recommended option.
4. If the owner changes the decision, record the new decision and its impact on timing/cost; do not accuse the owner in a group chat.
5. Propose a pilot delegation threshold for low-risk purchases, with monthly review, if leadership is open to process improvement.

**Suggested script:** “I want to make sure I follow the approval process correctly. For purchases of this type, should I treat the manager’s approval as final up to a defined amount, or should the owner approve each case? If owner approval is required, I’ll include the cash impact and options in the request so we avoid rework.”

**Transfer lesson:** Work with actual decision rights rather than pretending the org chart is the whole system. Preserve respect and accountability while making rework visible.

## Example 7: A senior colleague repeatedly takes credit

**User situation:** “My colleague presents my analysis to the owner as their own and gets praise. I want to expose them.”

**CPS diagnosis:** The key issue is contribution attribution and career visibility. Motive is uncertain; the colleague may be deliberately taking credit, may see the work as team output, or may not understand what was agreed. Establish the specific facts first.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Maintain a concise, authorized work log showing your deliverables, collaborators and business impact.
2. Send regular progress updates that include contribution by each person.
3. Correct the last incident privately if safe: describe the specific slide or decision and request an accurate project recap.
4. If it repeats, raise the pattern with the manager using examples, not labels.
5. Ask for explicit role ownership and a predictable forum to present the work you lead.

**Suggested script:** “In the review, the forecast model was described as your deliverable. I built the model and recommendation, and X provided the source data. Can we update the project note so the contribution record is accurate? For the next review, I’d also like to present the model since I own its assumptions and follow-up.”

**What not to do:** Do not spread counter-rumours, exaggerate your contribution, or try to make the colleague fail.

## Example 8: A real case is cited to prove an employee’s motive

**User situation:** “Tata–Mistry proves that every owner-led company only wants yes-men. Tell me how to beat the owner.”

**CPS diagnosis:** A prominent board dispute illustrates a conflict between leadership, board process and ownership influence; it does not establish a universal rule about owner-led companies or prove motives in the user’s firm.

**Recommended plan:**
1. Identify the user’s actual decision and company governance form.
2. Map formal authority, informal influence, decision evidence and the user’s current risk.
3. Look for observable signs: repeated reversals, unclear approval rights, or punishment for documented dissent.
4. Generate multiple explanations and test them through a respectful conversation or small low-risk request.
5. Recommend a process-based route (clear delegation, decision memo, sponsor consultation or formal escalation), depending on evidence.

**Transfer lesson:** Public cases are pattern libraries, not templates for mind-reading. Transfer mechanisms—such as informal veto or mandate ambiguity—only when the user’s facts support them.
